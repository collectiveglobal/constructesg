Blockchain in construction: what it actually does for ESG

Resources 4 min read

Blockchain gets described in a lot of ways, most of them more exciting than useful. For a construction project, the useful part is simple: it gives everyone involved a shared record that can't be quietly changed after the fact. When that record holds the evidence behind your ESG reporting, other people can trust what you report.

This article explains what that means in practice, where it helps, and where it doesn't.

The problem: ESG evidence is scattered and easy to dispute

A single building involves dozens of companies: the developer, the general contractor, trades, suppliers, manufacturers, consultants and inspectors. Each keeps its own records in its own format. Delivery tickets, mill certificates, product declarations, timesheets, site diaries, spreadsheets and email threads all hold part of the story.

When it's time to report on materials, emissions or labour practices, someone has to gather those records, reconcile them and vouch for them. If an assessor, lender or insurer asks where a number came from, the answer is often a folder of PDFs and the memory of whoever built the spreadsheet. Files get edited, overwritten or lost, and there is rarely a reliable way to show when a document was added or whether it has changed since.

What a blockchain ledger adds

A blockchain is a ledger shared across multiple computers. New entries are added in order, and each entry is cryptographically linked to the one before it. Changing an old entry would break that link, so tampering is visible to anyone who checks.

For project records, that gives you three properties that are hard to get any other way:

  • A fixed timeline. Every record carries a timestamp that can't be backdated, so you can show what was known and when.
  • Proof of integrity. A document's digital fingerprint, called a hash, is stored on the ledger. Anyone can later confirm that the file in front of them is the same one that was recorded.
  • One shared record. Developers, trades and suppliers add to the same history instead of keeping separate copies that drift apart.

What gets recorded

ConstructESG uses a ledger to anchor the records that ESG reporting depends on:

  • Materials: what was delivered, from which supplier and manufacturer, with its certificates and environmental product declarations.
  • Carbon: the quantities and emission factors behind each footprint estimate, so a figure can be traced back to its inputs. Our guide to carbon footprint estimation covers how those figures are built.
  • People and processes: the companies and workers involved, hours on site, and the approvals and inspections that took place.
  • Vendors: who supplied what, and the documentation they provided.

You don't need to understand the cryptography to use it. Your team uploads documents and enters data as it normally would, and the ledger works in the background.

What blockchain can't do

A ledger proves that a record hasn't changed since it was added. It can't prove the record was accurate when it went in. If a delivery ticket lists the wrong product, the blockchain will faithfully preserve the wrong product.

A ledger doesn't replace good record-keeping. It makes good record-keeping provable.

That's why the process around the technology matters as much as the technology itself:

  • Capture data as close to the source as possible (at delivery, installation and inspection) rather than reconstructing it at the end of a project.
  • Be clear about who is responsible for entering and approving each type of record.
  • Keep supporting documents attached to the data they support, so a reviewer can check both.

Who benefits

  • Developers and contractors spend less time assembling evidence and answering follow-up questions, and can stand behind the figures they report.
  • Assessors and regulators can check that the evidence behind a report hasn't been altered, without relying on the goodwill of the company being assessed.
  • Insurers and lenders get a clearer view of material quality and project governance when they price risk.

Key points

  • Blockchain gives project records a fixed timeline and makes tampering visible.
  • It proves records haven't changed, not that they were right to begin with, so capture data at the source.
  • ConstructESG uses it to anchor the material, carbon, labour and vendor records behind your ESG reports.
