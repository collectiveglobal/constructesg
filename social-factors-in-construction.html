The social side of ESG on construction projects

Social 4 min read

The environmental side of ESG gets most of the attention, partly because carbon is easy to put a number on. The social side is harder to measure, but in construction it matters just as much. The work is people-intensive, often dangerous, and relies on long chains of subcontractors and suppliers. How a company treats the people involved is a real business risk, and clients, investors and governments want evidence that it's being managed.

Health and safety

Safety is the most established social topic in construction, and the one most companies already track. ESG reviewers usually look for more than a clean record, though. They want to see how safety is managed:

  • Recordable incidents and lost-time injuries, tracked consistently and measured against hours worked.
  • Near-miss reporting, which shows whether problems are caught before they cause harm.
  • Training, orientations and certifications for everyone on site, including subcontractors.
  • Mental health and wellbeing, which the industry increasingly treats as a safety issue in its own right.

Subcontractor performance counts. On most projects, many of the people on site don't work directly for the general contractor, so a safety program that stops at your own employees misses much of the risk.

Fair labour practices

Fair treatment covers wages, hours, contracts, the right to raise concerns without retaliation, and being paid on time. Late payment is a persistent problem in construction, and several Canadian jurisdictions, including Ontario and the federal government, have introduced prompt payment legislation for construction contracts.

Records of hours, pay and payment timing across your subcontractors show that you take this seriously, and they help you spot problems early.

Supply-chain labour risks

Many construction materials and components are made overseas, sometimes in regions and industries with known risks of forced or child labour. Canadian law requires many larger companies to report annually on the steps they take to identify and reduce those risks in their supply chains.

Managing this starts with knowing who your suppliers are and where products come from, which is why material traceability is as much a social issue as an environmental one. From there, supplier codes of conduct, questionnaires and certifications help you show you've done your due diligence.

Diversity, inclusion and skills

Construction faces persistent skills shortages, and its workforce has historically drawn from a narrow pool. Companies are increasingly asked about workforce diversity, apprenticeships and training, and their use of diverse and Indigenous-owned businesses in the supply chain. Some public owners score these in procurement.

Communities around the site

Projects affect their neighbours through noise, dust, traffic and disruption, and they can also bring local jobs and investment. Community engagement, complaint tracking and local hiring all fall under the social heading. On projects that affect Indigenous lands or communities, early and meaningful engagement is essential.

Showing it, not just saying it

Most construction companies already care about these issues. What's usually missing is evidence. A policy says what a company intends. Records show what actually happened: who was on site, how many hours they worked, what training they had, which incidents occurred and how they were resolved.

A policy says what a company intends. Records show what actually happened.

Key points

  • The social side of ESG covers safety, fair labour, supply-chain risks, diversity and community impact.
  • Subcontractors and suppliers count: many of the people, and much of the risk, sit outside your own payroll.
  • Records of hours, training, incidents and suppliers turn good intentions into evidence.
