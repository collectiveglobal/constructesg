Why traceability actually matters in construction

Environmental 4 min read

Traceability means being able to follow a material from where it was made to where it ended up in a building, with the records to prove each step. On most projects that information exists only in fragments: on delivery tickets, in submittals, in the heads of site staff. Much of it is lost by handover.

That used to be an acceptable gap. It is becoming an expensive one.

What traceability covers

A traceable material has a record of:

  • Origin: who manufactured it, where, and from what inputs.
  • Specification: the product, grade and standard it was supplied to, with test reports or mill certificates.
  • Environmental data: its environmental product declaration (EPD) and any certifications, such as chain-of-custody certification for timber.
  • Movement: when it was shipped and delivered, and by whom.
  • Installation: where it was used in the building, and who inspected it.

Together these form the chain of custody: an unbroken sequence of records connecting each handoff.

Quality and safety

Material substitutions happen on every project, sometimes for good reasons and sometimes not. When a product fails or is recalled, the first question is where else it was used. Without traceability, answering that means opening up walls or relying on memory. With it, you can find every affected location from the record.

Traceability also makes it harder for non-conforming or counterfeit products to slip through, because a delivery that doesn't match its paperwork stands out.

Carbon

A building's embodied carbon depends on the specific products that went into it. Two concrete mixes of the same strength can have very different footprints depending on how much cement they contain. Steel made from recycled scrap in an electric arc furnace typically carries far less carbon than steel made from iron ore.

If you only know what was specified, not what was delivered, your carbon estimate is a guess. Traceability links each delivery to its EPD, so the as-built footprint reflects what was actually built. Our guide to carbon footprint estimation explains how those figures come together.

Supply-chain responsibility

Owners, investors and governments increasingly expect companies to know who is in their supply chain and how workers there are treated. In Canada, the Fighting Against Forced Labour and Child Labour in Supply Chains Act requires many larger businesses to report each year on the steps they take to reduce those risks. Construction supply chains, which often pass through several countries before a product reaches site, are part of that picture.

Traceability records give you the evidence to answer those questions, and they make it obvious when a supplier can't be accounted for.

Reporting and handover

ESG reports, green building certifications and public procurement increasingly ask for documented evidence rather than declarations. When records are captured as the project goes, reporting is a matter of pulling them together. When they aren't, it becomes a reconstruction exercise months after the fact.

At handover, a complete material record also helps the owner maintain, renovate and eventually deconstruct the building, including knowing which materials could be reused.

Where to start

You don't need to trace every fastener on day one. A practical approach:

  1. Start with high-impact materials. Concrete, reinforcing and structural steel, aluminium, glass, insulation and engineered timber usually account for most of a building's embodied carbon and much of its supply-chain risk.
  2. Capture at delivery. Record the supplier, product and quantity, and attach the certificates and EPD when the material arrives, not at project close.
  3. Link records to locations. Note where each batch was installed, even if only by floor or zone.
  4. Ask suppliers early. Make EPDs and certificates a condition of the purchase order so the data arrives with the product.
  5. Keep it in one place. A shared record that every party contributes to is far more useful than documents spread across inboxes.

ConstructESG is built around this workflow. Records are captured as the project progresses, linked to their supporting documents and anchored to a tamper-evident ledger, so they hold up when someone asks to see the evidence.

Key points

  • Traceability is the unbroken record of a material from manufacturer to installed location.
  • It protects quality, makes carbon estimates reflect what was actually built, and supports supply-chain reporting.
  • Start with the few materials that matter most, and capture records at delivery.
