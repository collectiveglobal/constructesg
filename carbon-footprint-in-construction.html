Carbon footprint estimation and ESG: a practical guide

Environmental 5 min read

A building's carbon footprint is an estimate of the greenhouse gases released across its life: making its materials, building it, running it for decades and eventually taking it apart. ESG reporting, green building certification and a growing number of public projects ask for that estimate, and it has to be defensible.

This guide covers what the footprint includes, how it's calculated, and the mistakes that most often undermine it.

Operational and embodied carbon

The footprint has two parts:

  • Operational carbon comes from the energy used to heat, cool, light and power the building while it's in use.
  • Embodied carbon comes from everything else: extracting and manufacturing materials, transporting them, construction, maintenance and replacement, and demolition and disposal. It's sometimes called capital carbon, because most of it is released before anyone moves in.

As buildings become more energy efficient and electricity grids get cleaner, operational emissions shrink and embodied carbon becomes a larger share of the total. Unlike operational carbon, it can't be reduced after the fact. Once the concrete is poured, its emissions have already happened.

The life-cycle stages

Whole-building life cycle assessment splits the footprint into stages, following the module structure of the European standard EN 15978 that most assessment tools and guidelines use:

  • A1–A3, product stage: raw material extraction, transport to the factory and manufacturing. This is usually the largest part of embodied carbon.
  • A4–A5, construction stage: transport to site and the construction process, including waste.
  • B, use stage: maintenance, repair and replacement, plus operational energy and water.
  • C, end of life: demolition, transport, processing and disposal.
  • D, beyond the building: potential benefits from reuse and recycling, reported separately.

Always state which stages an estimate covers. A figure for A1–A3 and a figure for A to C are not comparable.

How the estimate is calculated

At its core, the calculation is quantity multiplied by an emission factor, added up across every material and activity:

  • Quantities come from the drawings, the bill of quantities or the building information model, and later from what was actually delivered to site.
  • Emission factors give the carbon per unit of each material. The best source is a product-specific environmental product declaration (EPD) from the manufacturer. Where there isn't one, industry-average EPDs or generic databases fill the gap, usually with a conservative margin.

Results are expressed in kilograms or tonnes of CO2-equivalent (CO2e), which combines methane and other greenhouse gases into a single figure. Dividing by floor area (kgCO2e/m²) makes buildings of different sizes comparable.

Where most of the carbon is

On most projects a few materials dominate. Concrete, largely because of the cement in it, is usually the biggest single contributor, followed by steel and aluminium. Insulation, glazing and finishes add up too, especially across replacement cycles.

That's good news for accuracy: getting quantities and emission factors right for a handful of materials delivers most of it. It also shows where reductions come from, such as lower-cement concrete mixes, recycled-content steel, efficient structural design and reusing existing buildings and materials.

How the footprint fits into ESG reporting

Corporate climate reporting generally follows the Greenhouse Gas Protocol, which splits a company's emissions into three scopes:

  • Scope 1: direct emissions, such as fuel burned by your own equipment and vehicles.
  • Scope 2: emissions from the electricity and heat you buy.
  • Scope 3: everything else in your value chain, including the embodied carbon of the materials you purchase.

For a construction company, Scope 3 is usually by far the largest category, and it's the one clients and disclosure frameworks increasingly ask about. Project-level estimates feed straight into it: if each project records its materials and their emission factors, the company-wide figure becomes a roll-up rather than a separate exercise.

A number without its quantities, sources and assumptions can't be checked, and a number that can't be checked won't be trusted.

Common mistakes

  • Unclear boundaries. Not stating which life-cycle stages, building elements or scopes are included.
  • Estimating once. A design-stage estimate is useful for decisions, but it isn't what was built. Substitutions during procurement can change the result significantly, so update the estimate with as-delivered data. Material traceability makes that possible.
  • Generic data where specific data exists. Industry averages hide the difference between a low-carbon product and a high-carbon one, which is exactly the difference you want credit for.
  • No audit trail. Quantities, sources and assumptions need to stay attached to the result.

ConstructESG keeps each carbon figure connected to the quantities, EPDs and delivery records behind it, so estimates can be updated as the project progresses and checked by anyone who needs to.

Key points

  • A building's footprint combines operational and embodied carbon across its whole life.
  • The calculation is quantity × emission factor; accuracy depends on good quantities and product-specific EPDs.
  • State your boundaries, update the estimate with as-built data, and keep the audit trail.
