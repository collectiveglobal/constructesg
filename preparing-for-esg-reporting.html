Getting ready for ESG reporting: a step-by-step plan

Getting started 4 min read

ESG reporting rarely fails because of the report itself. It fails because the data behind it is missing, scattered or impossible to verify when the deadline arrives. The companies that find it manageable treat reporting as a by-product of how they run projects, not a separate exercise at year end.

Here is a practical plan for getting there.

1. Work out what applies to you

Start with the requests you already receive. Tender documents, client questionnaires, lender conditions and certification schemes all spell out what they want to know. Add any disclosure rules that apply to your company directly, based on its size, its ownership, or the public bodies it works for.

Write these down in one list, with who is asking, what they want and when. That list becomes the scope of your reporting, and it stops you from measuring things nobody needs.

2. Decide what matters most

Not every ESG topic matters equally to every company. A materiality assessment ranks topics by how much they affect your business and how much your business affects them. For most construction companies, embodied carbon, health and safety, supply-chain practices and governance controls come out near the top. Focus your effort there first.

3. Map the data you already have

Much of the information you need already exists somewhere in the business:

  • Material quantities in purchase orders, delivery tickets and bills of quantities.
  • Fuel and electricity use in invoices and equipment logs.
  • Hours worked, training and incidents in timesheets and safety records.
  • Supplier details and certificates in procurement files.

For each item on your list, note where the data lives, who owns it, what format it's in and how complete it is. The gaps you find become your to-do list.

4. Set your boundaries and baseline

Decide which parts of the business and which projects your reporting will cover, and pick a baseline year to measure progress against. Write down the methods you'll use, such as which emission factors and life-cycle stages count in a carbon estimate. Consistent boundaries matter more than perfect ones: numbers that change method every year can't be compared.

5. Assign responsibility

Every data point needs an owner: someone who collects it, someone who checks it, and a date it's due. On projects, that often means site managers and project administrators, not just a sustainability lead. Build these tasks into existing routines, such as delivery checks, weekly reports and project close-out, so they don't depend on anyone remembering.

6. Capture data as projects run

The biggest improvement most companies can make is to record ESG data at the source, as it happens. A certificate attached to a delivery on the day it arrives takes seconds. The same certificate chased from a supplier eighteen months later can take weeks. Capturing data this way also creates the audit trail that reviewers will ask for.

Treat reporting as a by-product of how you run projects, not a separate exercise at year end.

7. Report, review and improve

Your first report won't be perfect, and it doesn't need to be. Be clear about your methods and limitations, then use what you learned to close gaps before the next cycle. Many companies eventually seek independent assurance of their figures, which is far easier when the evidence has been kept from the start.

  • List who is asking for what before you start measuring.
  • Most of the data already exists: map where it lives and who owns it.
  • Capture data at the source as projects run, and keep the evidence with it.
