Governance in construction: controls that stand up to scrutiny

Governance 3 min read

Governance is the least visible part of ESG and often the most important. It covers how a company makes decisions, how those decisions are checked, and how problems are caught and dealt with. For construction companies, good governance is what makes everything else believable: a carbon figure or a safety record is only as trustworthy as the process that produced it.

Why governance is harder in construction

Projects involve large sums of money, many parties and long supply chains. Decisions are made quickly, often on site, by people under schedule pressure, and changes are constant. Each of those creates room for error, waste and, occasionally, wrongdoing.

Clients, lenders, insurers and public owners know this, which is why they look closely at how contractors manage procurement, approvals and records.

The controls that matter most

  • Procurement and tendering. Clear, documented processes for selecting suppliers and subcontractors, with competitive bidding where appropriate and a record of why each award was made.
  • Conflicts of interest. A requirement to declare personal or financial relationships with suppliers, and a record of how each declared conflict was handled.
  • Anti-bribery and corruption. Policies on gifts, hospitality and dealings with officials, backed by training and a way to report concerns.
  • Approvals and change control. Defined authority levels for spending, variations and design changes, so every significant decision has a named approver and a date.
  • Document control. One current version of drawings, specifications and contracts, with a history of what changed and when.
  • Subcontractor and supplier vetting. Checks on insurance, safety performance, financial stability and ESG practices before work begins, and monitoring while it's under way.
  • Whistleblowing. A confidential channel for workers and subcontractors to raise concerns without fear of retaliation.

Oversight at the top

At company level, governance also covers who is accountable for ESG. Reviewers look for clear ownership, such as a named executive or board committee, regular reporting to leadership, and ESG risks included in the company's wider risk management. For smaller companies this doesn't need to be elaborate, but it should be written down and followed.

The evidence problem

Most construction companies have governance policies. Fewer can prove those policies were followed on a specific project. When an assessor asks who approved a material substitution, or whether a subcontractor's certificates were checked before they started work, the answer often depends on searching through email.

A policy says what should happen. A record shows what did.

This is where governance and record-keeping meet. Approvals, inspections, certificates and changes captured as they happen, each with a timestamp and a name attached, give you an audit trail that answers those questions in minutes.

Making it practical

  1. Start with your highest risks. For most contractors, that's procurement, change control and subcontractor vetting.
  2. Keep processes simple enough to follow on site. A control nobody uses is worse than none, because it creates false confidence.
  3. Record decisions where the work happens, not in a separate system that gets updated later.
  4. Review regularly. Check a sample of projects each year to confirm the controls are working, and fix what isn't.

Key points

  • Governance is how decisions are made, checked and recorded, and it underpins the credibility of your other ESG data.
  • Focus on procurement, conflicts of interest, approvals, document control and subcontractor vetting.
  • Policies aren't enough: keep records that show the controls were followed on each project.
